BP-104 · Aeronautics

Propeller

Fixed-pitch, two-blade, forged-aluminium propeller of a four-seat light aircraft, Ø1905 mm, with its spinner and its bolting to the crankshaft flange: the blade from ahead, four blade sections and the hub in section

A propeller is a rotating wing: each blade element meets a relative wind of forward speed and its own speed, and its lift, tipped forward, is the thrust

At every station the blade meets a relative wind made of the forward speed plus the inflow it draws (axial) and its own speed ωr (tangential): the velocity triangle. The angle of attack is the blade angle less the helix angle, α = β − φ. Standing still at full throttle the triangles are flat, α is large and the root is stalled; climbing at 74 kt α is moderate; cruising at 110 kt it is small and the propeller is 85 % efficient; gliding with the throttle closed φ passes β − α₀ and the blades push back on the air: the propeller windmills. Thrust, power and efficiency against advance ratio J = V/nD come from blade-element momentum theory with a thin-aerofoil lift slope and a stall cap.

Gravity is the pump: the height of the fuel above the carburettor pushes it through the lines, and a vent must let air in for every litre that leaves

Each wing tank's surface stands about 1.36 m above the carburettor's float valve, so ρgh puts 9.6 kPa behind the fuel with no pump at all. The lines lose only a fraction of a kilopascal at the 38 L/h cruise flow (Hagen–Poiseuille, turning turbulent at Re 2900), so the float valve could pass 86 L/h wide open, more than 1.5 times the take-off flow. On BOTH the two tanks feed in parallel like communicating vessels, and a slight sideslip makes one feed faster until their surfaces stand level in the tilted gravity. Select LEFT and the rotor's window leaves the right port. Block the left vent and Boyle's law takes over: every litre out lowers the ullage's pressure until the vacuum cancels the head, the flow falls below demand, the float bowl empties and the engine starves; the right tank brings it back. BP-112 · Aeronautics

Elevator trim tab

Elevator trim tab system of a four-seat high-wing light aircraft: the trim wheel, cockpit drum and position indicator in the pedestal, the trim cable loop and its pulleys, the jackscrew actuator in the port elevator (grooved cable drum on the screw, Acme screw, bronze nut, ball bearing, slotted housing, stops and bush), the push-pull rod, the tab's horn, the piano hinge and the tab

A small tab at the elevator's trailing edge has the longest lever about the hinge, so a few degrees of tab hold the whole elevator where the flight needs it, and a self-locking screw holds the tab

The air's moment about the elevator's hinge is what the pilot holds: slow from 110 to 65 kt and the elevator must come up 4.7°, so the air pushes it back down and the pilot pulls 22 N. Roll the trim wheel 2.2 turns nose up and the cable loop turns the actuator's drum and Tr10×2 screw; the bronze nut runs 6.6 mm forward, the rod pulls the tab's horn and the tab goes down from −3.9° to +7.9°. The air on the tab now pushes it up, and that moment, 3.1 % of the elevator's area at the end of its chord, cancels the elevator's own: let go and the elevator floats exactly where it is held, and the pull is gone. The screw's lead angle is 4.0° and its friction angle 8.5°, so the air load on the tab lies inside the friction cone and cannot turn the screw back: the tab stays set with no lock. Disconnect the rod and nothing holds it: the tab floats, then flutters from stop to stop.

BP-107 · Aeronautics

Wing flaps

Electrically driven single-slotted flaps of a four-seat high-wing light aircraft: the wing cut through the inboard flap track, the 28 V jackscrew actuator in section, the bellcranks, push-pull rods and crossed cables in plan, and the cockpit selector with its follow-up switches

A curved track turns one push into two motions: first the flap slides aft and the wing grows, then it turns down and opens a slot that keeps the air on the flap

Each flap rides on two rollers in one curved slot. While both rollers are on the slot's straight part the flap only slides aft (Fowler motion: the chord grows up to 5 %); when the slot curves, the aft roller drops ahead of the forward one and the flap turns down, opening a gap of 2 % of the chord between its nose and the shroud. Air from under the wing jets through that gap over the flap, starting a fresh boundary layer that stays attached at 30°, so the lift curve moves up (C_L,max 1.46 to 1.78) and the wing stalls at a lower angle and a lower speed (53 to 48 kt). A 28 V motor turns a self-locking Tr16×4 jackscrew through 25:1 spur gears; the nut swings a bellcrank whose rod drives the starboard flap and whose crossed cables drive the port bellcrank. A follow-up cable turns the selector's switch carrier, so the motor stops when the flaps reach the selected angle; limit switches stop it at 0° and 30°; full travel takes 9 s.

  • Pilot training: what each flap setting does to lift, drag, attitude and stall speed, why the flaps take 9 seconds, why there is a flap limit speed
  • Aeronautical courses: Fowler and slotted flaps, the slot effect on the boundary layer, C_L against α, the stall speed from C_L,max
  • Machine design: a two-roller track as a cam, a self-locking jackscrew, a mechanical follow-up servo and limit switches, a crossed-cable interconnect

BP-108 · Aeronautics

Pitot-static system

Pitot-static system of a four-seat high-wing light aircraft: the heated pitot head under the left wing with its ram hole, drain hole, water dam and heater; the two flush static ports on the tail cone, cross-connected; the lines, the drain traps and the alternate static valve; and the airspeed indicator, the altimeter and the vertical speed indicator, each in section and from the front with half its dial broken away to show the capsule, rocking shaft, sector, pinion and hairspring

Three instruments read two pressures: total minus static is airspeed, static alone is height, and static against a deliberately leaky copy of itself is climb rate

The pitot head stops the air, so its line holds total pressure, static plus about 1.97 kPa at 110 kt; the airspeed indicator's capsule feels only that difference and reads it on the sea-level law, so at 6500 ft the same 110 kt is 121 kt true. The altimeter's evacuated capsules swell as static pressure falls and its dial is the standard atmosphere's pressure-height law, set to the day's pressure with the Kollsman knob, about 27 ft a hectopascal. The VSI's case leaks to static through a Ø0.25 mm capillary, a first-order lag with τ = 8μLV/πr⁴p of 5.5 s at sea level, so a steady climb holds a steady Δp = ρgwτ. Block the ram hole and the open drain lets the pitot side fall to static: the ASI reads zero. Block the drain too and the trapped air turns the ASI into an altimeter, reading high in a climb. Ice the static ports and the altimeter freezes, the VSI sinks to zero and the ASI reads high in a descent; the alternate source brings them back from the cabin, a little low in pressure, so they read about 4 kt and 50 ft high.

  • Pilot training: what each instrument senses, why IAS and TAS part with height, and the classic pitot and static blockages and their symptoms
  • Engineering courses: the standard atmosphere, impact pressure and compressible CAS, aneroid altimetry, a laminar capillary as an RC time constant, orifice flow
  • A software-concept explainer: a derivative computed by comparing a signal with a lagged copy of itself (the VSI is a high-pass filter), and a sensor that fails silent or fails plausible

BP-109 · Aeronautics

Gyro instruments

Gyroscopic flight instruments of a four-seat high-wing light aircraft and their vacuum system: the engine-driven dry pump with carbon vanes, the regulator, the central air filter and the suction gauge; the air-driven attitude indicator cut across its gyro with its pendulous erection vanes, the air-driven heading indicator in plan on its spin axis with its card and setting knob, and the electric turn coordinator cut square to its canted gimbal, with its ball

A fast rotor holds its direction in space, H = Iω: a torque only turns it slowly, at τ/H, so it can carry a horizon and a heading; take away its air and it drifts and topples

A 52 cm³ carbon-vane pump on the engine's accessory pad draws about 53 L/min of cabin air through the central filter and the jets of the two vacuum gyros, and a spring-loaded regulator lets outside air in to hold the suction near 5 inHg (4.1 at idle). The jets spin the attitude gyro's 0.17 kg brass rotor to 21 000 rpm, H = 0.11 N·m·s, so its residual torque wanders it only 0.5°/min while its pendulous vanes unbalance the exhaust jets and erect it at 5.9°/min toward the apparent vertical, which in a turn is tilted (the turn error). The heading gyro, H = 0.065, drifts 2.5° in 15 minutes from its pivots and 11.5°/h more from the Earth's rotation at 50° N, so the pilot resets it against the magnetic compass with the knob. The electric turn coordinator's gimbal is canted 30°, so it feels roll as well as yaw; its rotor's H against a spring gives a deflection proportional to rate of turn, 20° at the standard 3°/s, which at the parent sheet's 110 kt cruise is a 16.8° bank. Its ball lies where gravity and the turn's centripetal pull balance: centred when coordinated, outside in a skid, inside in a slip. When the pump fails the vacuum rotors run down over minutes: as H falls the same small torques turn them faster, the horizon wanders and lies on its stops and the heading creeps, while the electric turn coordinator keeps showing the turn.

  • Pilot training: what each gyro instrument senses, why the heading indicator must be reset, the attitude indicator's turn error, partial-panel flying on the turn coordinator after a vacuum failure, and the ball in a skid and a slip
  • Physics and engineering courses: angular momentum and rigidity, precession Ω = τ/H, the apparent drift from the Earth's rotation, spring-restrained rate gyros, jet turbines, rotary-vane pumps and pressure regulation
  • A software-concept explainer: a reference that holds its state only while it is powered, slowly corrected toward a noisy measurement (erection toward the apparent vertical is a complementary filter), and a backup sensor on an independent supply

BP-110 · Aeronautics

Electrical system

28 V DC electrical system of a four-seat high-wing light aircraft: the belt-driven alternator with its claw-pole rotor, field winding, slip rings, brushes, three-phase stator and six-diode rectifier, the switching voltage regulator, the 24 V lead-acid battery, the split BAT / ALT master switch, the battery and starter contactors, the starter with its Bendix pinion at the ring gear, the main and avionics buses, the centre-zero ammeter, the low-voltage annunciator and a push-pull thermal breaker for every circuit

Two sources share one bus: the alternator holds it at 28 V by switching its own field, the battery fills every gap the alternator leaves, and a breaker's heat decides when a circuit has drawn too much

The alternator's rotor is an electromagnet: a few amps through its field winding, by way of two slip rings and brushes, make fourteen claw poles that sweep past a three-phase stator, and six diodes turn the result into DC. Its EMF and its winding's reactance both grow with speed, so it cuts in near 600 engine rpm at full field and can never give much more than about 90 A however fast it turns. The regulator holds the bus at 28.0 V by switching the field on and off at whatever duty cycle the load needs. The 24 V, 13.6 Ah battery has an open-circuit voltage of 12 × (SG + 0.84) and about 22 mΩ inside: cranking draws 330 A for an instant and sags the bus to 16.6 V, then 110 to 150 A while the Bendix pinion turns the engine over at some 180 rpm. With the alternator on line the battery charges, fast at first and then slower as its plates fill; when the belt breaks, the bus falls to the battery's 24.2 V, the low-voltage light comes on, and at 20 A the battery has about 35 minutes left, nearer three hours once the loads are shed. A chafed nav light wire shorts at 15 A through its 5 A breaker, whose bimetal heats along an inverse-time curve and trips it in about two and a half seconds.

  • Pilot and maintenance training: what the split master, the ammeter's sign and the low-voltage light mean, why the bus sags when cranking, what to shed after an alternator failure and how long the battery lasts
  • Electrical engineering courses: the claw-pole alternator and its self-limiting output, rectification, field-switching regulation, lead-acid charge acceptance and Peukert's law, DC motor inrush, inverse-time thermal protection
  • A software-concept explainer: a primary and a backup supply on one bus, graceful degradation and load shedding, and a circuit breaker that trips on accumulated overload rather than one bad sample

The circuit breaker pattern: closed, open, half-open

A bimetal heated by its own I²R bends until it knocks the latch out, so an overload trips after seconds along an inverse-time curve, while above 4 × In the coil's plunger strikes at once. Airframe

A strut turns a long cantilever into a short propped beam: the wing's bending moment, and the spar it needs, fall to a fifth
